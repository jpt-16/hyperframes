Before PitchCom, calling one pitch could involve an entire secret code.

For over a century, catchers communicated with pitchers using hand signals hidden between their legs.

One finger might mean a fastball, two might mean a curveball, and three might mean a slider.

But there was a problem.

The opposing team could sometimes steal those signs, especially when a runner reached second base and could see the catcher’s signals.

So teams developed different systems to hide their calls.

Sometimes, the catcher would use a specific sign sequence, where only the first, second, or third sign actually mattered.

Other teams used an indicator sign, or even changed the system depending on the inning or number of outs.

The goal was simple: make the signs look random to the other team while keeping them understandable to the pitcher.

Then came PitchCom.

Introduced in MLB in 2022, it allowed catchers to press buttons on a wrist device and send the pitch call directly to a receiver in the pitcher’s cap.

No more complicated finger sequences. No more worrying about a runner at second stealing the signs.

A century of hand signals, replaced by a few buttons.
